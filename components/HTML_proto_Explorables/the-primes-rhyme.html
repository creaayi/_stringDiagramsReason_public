The Primes
Rhyme

Why prime numbers aren't random — and how folding a table makes their hidden order visible.

Act I

A table with a secret

Write the integers 1 through 36 in a table with 6 columns. Just a simple grid.

Now ask: which of these numbers can possibly be prime? A prime has no divisors except 1 and itself. So anything divisible by 2 or 3 is immediately out.

prime candidate

Every number divisible by 2 sits in columns 2, 4, 6. Every multiple of 3 sits in column 3. What's left can only be in columns 1 and 5.

The observation: In a mod-6 table, prime candidates cluster exclusively in columns 1 and 5. Not randomly scattered — geometrically constrained.
Act II

Now fold it

Here's Mohammed's key insight — but to see it, the table has to be redrawn. Instead of rows running 1 to 6, centre each row on a multiple of 6. Now every row has a middle, and the gold candidates sit symmetrically on either side of it: one step below, one step above.

That is what 6k ± 1 means. Fold each row at its centre, and the two gold columns land exactly on top of each other.

This isn't coincidence. The two candidates are 6k−1 and 6k+1 — the same distance from the centre, on opposite sides. Folding at the centre maps each onto the other, because they are the same residue class, mirrored. The sheet is drawn translucent as it lands, so you can see both layers at once.

The symmetry: The prime candidates in mod-6 come in mirror pairs, ±1 from every multiple of 6. Folding makes this literally visible — two columns become one, which is φ(6)/2 = 1.
Act III

The same fold, one prime later

Mod 6 used the primes 2 and 3. Bring in 5 and the unit becomes 30 — and the picture below is drawn exactly as before, each row centred on a multiple of 30, candidates marked in gold.

There are now eight of them per unit, and they are still perfectly mirrored: 30k ± 1, ± 7, ± 11, ± 13. Fold at the centre and eight columns become four. That is φ(30)/2.

Only the gold cells are labelled; the rest of each row is already gone, struck by 2, 3 or 5. Notice 49, 77 and 91 sitting there in gold. They survived this sieve — they are not divisible by 2, 3 or 5 — but they are not prime either. Something still has to remove them.

Act III — continued

What the next prime costs

Here is the engine of the whole thing. To build the unit of 210, you take the eight survivors of mod 30 and repeat them seven times — once for each block of 30 that fits inside 210. Eight columns, seven copies: fifty-six candidates.

Then 7 arrives and strikes its own multiples. Watch where they fall.

8 columns × 7 copies = 56 candidates

Exactly one cell in every column. Not two in one and none in another — one each, all eight, every time. The new prime takes its toll evenly across the residue classes, which is why no column ever gets starved of primes while its neighbours keep theirs.

56 − 8 = 48. And φ(210) = 48. The count isn't a coincidence to be checked afterwards; it is what the repetition and the single strike leave behind.
Act III — coda

And it never stops

Every new prime does the same thing: the previous unit is copied, and the newcomer strikes once per column. The candidates thin out, the units grow, and the mirror symmetry survives all of it.

Mod 6 = 2×3. Two prime candidates per row, folding into 1 column.

The density falls towards zero and the structure never loosens. At every level the candidates come in mirror pairs, and every level is built from the one before it by copy-and-strike. This is the sense in which the primes rhyme.

Act IV

Where primes actually land

Now let's drop the actual primes onto the mod-6 table. Every prime greater than 3 must live in a gold column — but do all gold cells contain primes?

The distinction that matters: Every prime > 3 lives in a gold column. But most gold cells are composite. The table tells you where primes can be. It doesn't tell you which candidates are prime. Those are two different questions.

This is the honest boundary of the structural rhyme. The sieve constrains the territory. It doesn't fully determine the inhabitants.

What this means

The rhyme is real

What Mohammed's tables reveal is genuine: primes are not sprinkled randomly across the integers. They are structurally constrained — forced into specific columns at every primorial level, and those columns have a perfect folding symmetry that makes the constraint literally visible.

This is connected to deep mathematics: the wheel factorization, the Chinese Remainder Theorem, Euler's totient function. The symmetry under folding is not an observation — it's a theorem. It can be proved.

The formula: At primorial level p# (product of primes up to p), there are φ(p#) prime candidates per cycle, where φ is Euler's totient. They come in mirror pairs, so after folding: φ(p#) / 2 distinct columns. For mod 6: φ(6)/2 = 1. For mod 30: φ(30)/2 = 4. For mod 210: φ(210)/2 = 24.

Mohammed's paper goes further — proposing that this structure resolves several major open problems in number theory. Those claims await peer review. But the visual phenomenon he identified is beautiful, true, and underexplored as a teaching tool.

The fold animation you just played with is, to my knowledge, the first interactive version of this idea. Mohammed envisioned it as physical transparent layers — sheets of paper folding over each other. That image is exactly right.
